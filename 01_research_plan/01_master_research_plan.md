# Phase-wise Research Plan

## 0. Working title and framing

### Working title
**AI & the Student: Learning Accelerator or Learning Substitute?**

Alternative titles:
- **When AI Studies With You — and When It Studies Instead of You**
- **The Student-AI Balance**
- **Faster Learning, Weaker Thinking? Investigating AI's Student Impact**

### Topic / issue
The impact of generative AI on higher-education students' study behaviour, perceived learning, productivity, dependency, skill retention, anxiety and academic practices.

### Scope
Primary focus: higher-education students.
Geographic strategy: use global/UK evidence for broad context, plus an India/Gujarat component if primary research is collected locally.
Do not mix populations without labelling them.

### Communication objective
Help a student/educator understand the **different ways AI use can shape the student experience**, rather than telling the audience that AI is inherently good or bad.

### Intended audience
Primary: college/university students aged roughly 18–25.
Secondary: educators/design faculty interested in responsible AI-supported learning.

---

# 1. Research questions

## Main question
**How is generative AI changing the way students study, learn and complete academic work?**

## Quantitative questions
1. How common is GenAI use among students?
2. Which academic tasks are students most likely to use AI for?
3. Does greater reported AI-use intensity coincide with higher perceived AI dependency?
4. How does AI-use intensity relate to traditional study time?
5. How do AI-use levels relate to reported skill retention?
6. How do AI-use levels relate to exam anxiety?
7. How do different use cases (e.g. ideation vs direct answer generation) differ in reported outcomes?
8. Do students at different study stages or majors show different patterns?
9. Does institutional policy appear associated with usage patterns?
10. Is there evidence of a gap between AI access/skill and institutional support?

## Qualitative questions
1. What do students believe AI makes easier?
2. What do students feel they are learning better because of AI?
3. What tasks do students feel AI can replace rather than support?
4. When do students feel they stop thinking for themselves?
5. What makes students trust or distrust AI output?
6. How do students define “using AI responsibly”?
7. What fears or anxieties do students associate with AI?
8. What do students want universities/teachers to teach them about AI?

---

# 2. Research design

Use a **mixed-method design**:

### Quantitative
A. Supplied 50,000-row dataset for exploratory analysis.
B. Published external surveys/reports for real-world benchmarking.
C. A small primary student survey (recommended: 100–200 responses) to make the project locally relevant.

### Qualitative
A. Open-ended questions inside the primary survey.
B. 8–12 semi-structured student interviews OR two small focus groups.
C. Published qualitative evidence such as Jisc student discussions.

### Why mixed methods?
Numbers can show **how often** a pattern occurs. Student responses can explain **why the pattern may exist** and what the numbers mean in lived experience.

# 3. Primary survey plan

## Target
College/university students.

## Suggested sample
Aim for **100–200 usable responses**. If time is limited, 75+ can still be useful for exploratory student research, but report the sample size honestly.

## Sampling
Convenience/purposive sampling through:
- classmates;
- other college departments;
- student communities;
- friends at other colleges/universities.

Record:
- institution type;
- field/major;
- year of study;
- age band (optional);
- whether the student has access to paid AI tools.

Avoid collecting names, phone numbers or unnecessary identifiers.

## Survey structure

### A. Student profile
- Year of study
- Broad field/major
- Institution type
- Approximate weekly study hours

### B. AI usage
- Do you use generative AI for academic work? Yes/No
- How often? Never / Monthly / Weekly / Several times a week / Daily
- Approximate hours per week
- Tools used: ChatGPT / Gemini / Copilot / Claude / Perplexity / Other
- Main academic uses: explain concepts / summarise / brainstorm / writing/editing / solve problems / coding / generate answers / research / other

### C. Perceived benefits — 1 to 5 Likert
Rate:
- AI saves me time.
- AI helps me understand difficult concepts.
- AI helps me organise my study.
- AI helps me start tasks when I do not know where to begin.
- AI improves the quality of my academic work.
- AI makes feedback more accessible.

### D. Potential downsides — 1 to 5 Likert
Rate:
- I rely on AI before trying to solve a problem myself.
- I sometimes accept an AI answer without checking it.
- Using AI can reduce the effort I put into an assignment.
- I worry that AI is weakening some of my skills.
- I find it harder to know whether an answer is trustworthy.
- I feel pressure to use AI because other students use it.

### E. Learning / thinking
- When using AI, do you usually: learn with the explanation / use it to check your own work / combine AI and independent work / copy/adapt the answer / other
- “AI helps me think more deeply about a topic.” 1–5
- “AI sometimes replaces my own thinking.” 1–5
- “I can complete academic work faster with AI.” 1–5

### F. Institutional context
- Does your institution have clear AI rules?
- Are students taught how to use AI responsibly?
- Is AI encouraged, allowed with conditions, unclear, or prohibited?
- Do you feel prepared to use AI ethically in future work?

### G. Open-ended qualitative questions
1. **What is the biggest positive change AI has made to your student life?**
2. **What is the biggest negative change AI has made to your student life?**
3. **Describe a time when AI genuinely helped you learn something.**
4. **Describe a time when using AI made you feel you were learning less or thinking less.**
5. **What is one rule or skill universities should teach students about AI?**

# 4. Interview plan

Interview 8–12 students with different:
- AI-use frequencies;
- majors;
- years of study;
- attitudes toward AI.

## Interview prompts
1. Walk me through the last time you used AI for studying.
2. What did you ask it to do?
3. What did you do before and after receiving the answer?
4. Did it save time? What did you do with the saved time?
5. Have you ever rejected an AI answer? Why?
6. Have you ever copied/adapted an AI answer? What influenced that decision?
7. Do you think your ability to write, solve problems or remember information has changed?
8. What is the difference, in your mind, between “using AI as a tutor” and “using AI as a shortcut”?
9. Does your college/university make the rules clear?
10. If your institution could change one thing about AI and education, what should it be?

Do not lead participants toward “AI is bad”. Ask neutral questions and code both positive and negative experiences.

# 5. External quantitative evidence

Use these as **benchmarks**, not as substitutes for your own data.

### HEPI 2026
1,054 full-time UK undergraduates surveyed by Savanta in December 2025. HEPI reports:
- 95% used AI in at least one way.
- 94% used GenAI for assessed work.
- 12% directly included AI-generated text in assessed work.
- 49% felt AI improved their student experience.
- 16% felt it worsened their student experience.
- 68% considered AI skills essential to thrive.

Source:
https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2026/

### HEPI 2025
1,041 full-time UK undergraduates:
- 92% used AI in some form.
- 88% used GenAI for assessments.
- 51% cited saving time as a reason for use.
- 50% cited improving work quality.
- 36% had received institutional AI-skills training.

Source:
https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2025/

### Jisc 2025
Jisc combined discussion groups with 173 students and seven surveys totaling 1,274 student responses. Themes include academic integrity, privacy, misinformation, employability, anxiety and the need for practical institutional support.

Source:
https://www.jisc.ac.uk/reports/student-perceptions-of-ai-2025

### India / Gujarat context
A 2025 study of 200 university students in Assam and Gujarat reported that 59.1% used GenAI for academic purposes; the study also reported mixed perceptions of benefits and concerns.

Source:
https://bodhijournals.com/index.php/bijrhas/article/view/4

### Indian institutional context
The EY-Parthenon/FICCI 2025 survey covered 30 leading Indian HEIs. It reported that roughly 57% had AI-related policies and that 60% permitted student use of AI tools. This is institutional evidence, not a student survey.

Source:
https://www.ey.com/en_in/insights/education/harnessing-ai-in-higher-education-opportunities-and-the-road-ahead

# 6. Supplied 50,000-row dataset: how to use it

The supplied dataset contains:
- 50,000 rows;
- 16 original variables;
- GPA before and after semester;
- weekly GenAI hours;
- traditional study hours;
- primary use case;
- prompting skill;
- tool diversity;
- subscription status;
- perceived dependency;
- institutional policy;
- exam anxiety;
- skill retention;
- burnout risk.

## Quality checks performed
- Missing values: none.
- Duplicate rows: none.
- Duplicate Student_ID values: none.
- GPA values fall within the supplied 1–4 range.
- Dependency and anxiety values fall within 1–10.
- Skill retention falls within 0–100.
- Weekly GenAI hours fall within 0–40.

## Important provenance limitation
No source, survey instrument, sampling frame or collection date is embedded in the supplied CSV. Therefore:
**do not present the 50,000 rows as a real-world representative survey unless you obtain its provenance.**

# 7. First exploratory patterns from the supplied dataset

These are **descriptive observations from the supplied file**, not causal findings.

### AI-use intensity and dependency
Mean perceived AI dependency rises across GenAI-use bands:
- 0–2 h/week: about 2.45
- >2–5 h: about 2.78
- >5–10 h: about 3.30
- >10–20 h: about 4.30
- >20 h: about 6.37

### AI-use intensity and exam anxiety
Mean exam anxiety also rises across these bands:
- 0–2 h/week: about 3.81
- >2–5 h: about 3.88
- >5–10 h: about 4.07
- >10–20 h: about 4.79
- >20 h: about 5.62

### AI-use intensity and skill retention
The overall Pearson correlation between weekly GenAI hours and skill-retention score is approximately **-0.118** in this dataset.

### AI-use intensity and traditional study
The correlation between weekly GenAI hours and traditional study hours is approximately **-0.157**.

### Dependency and usage
The correlation between weekly GenAI hours and perceived AI dependency is approximately **0.665**.

### GPA caution
Pre-semester and post-semester GPA are strongly correlated (about **0.927**), so post-semester GPA should not be interpreted as an isolated AI effect. Use GPA change carefully and consider baseline GPA.

# 8. Analysis sequence

Do not jump straight to one dramatic chart.

## Analysis 1 — Adoption
How much and how often do students use AI?

## Analysis 2 — Purpose
What are students using AI for?

## Analysis 3 — Behaviour
Does AI use coincide with changes in traditional study time?

## Analysis 4 — Dependency
At what usage levels does perceived dependency rise?

## Analysis 5 — Learning
How does reported skill retention vary with AI-use intensity and use case?

## Analysis 6 — Emotional experience
How do anxiety/burnout indicators vary?

## Analysis 7 — AI literacy
Does prompt skill/tool diversity correspond to different usage patterns?

## Analysis 8 — Institutional context
How do policy categories relate to usage patterns?

## Analysis 9 — Student profiles
Create descriptive profiles such as:
- low-use / independent;
- balanced AI-assisted;
- high-use / high-dependency.

These are analytic profiles, not diagnoses or labels.

## Analysis 10 — Compare with real-world published evidence
Check whether patterns in the supplied/primary data align with or differ from published studies.

Rules, training, access, inconsistent guidance.

Differences by discipline, access, skill and confidence.

# 10. Story directions to explore

## Direction 1 — The AI Study Spectrum
**Question:** What changes as students move from occasional AI assistance to heavy AI use?
Visuals: distribution, slope/band charts, dependency/retention comparisons.
Audience relevance: directly relatable to students.

## Direction 2 — Tutor or Shortcut?
**Question:** What are students actually asking AI to do?
Visuals: use-case composition + qualitative quotes/themes.
Potential interaction: select a use case and reveal associated student experiences.

## Direction 3 — Faster, But What Gets Replaced?
**Question:** Does increased AI use coexist with reduced traditional study time and skill retention?
Visuals: scatterplots, bands, paired comparisons.
Important caveat: association, not causation.

## Direction 4 — The AI Support Gap
**Question:** Are institutions keeping pace with how students use AI?
Visuals: student use vs institutional guidance/training.
Use HEPI/Jisc/EDUCAUSE and primary survey evidence.

## Direction 5 — Not One Student, Not One AI Experience
**Question:** How different are student AI experiences?
Visuals: student profiles/segments based on usage, literacy, dependency and study behaviour.
This is useful if the data supports meaningful clusters.

## Direction 6 — AI: Better Experience, Mixed Consequences
**Question:** How can students simultaneously experience AI as helpful and harmful?
Visuals: diverging bars / two-sided evidence / qualitative quote cards.
This direction most directly captures the project's central tension.

---

# 11. Evaluation matrix for choosing the final story

Score each direction internally on:
- Evidence strength
- Data availability
- Quantitative depth
- Qualitative depth
- Audience relevance
- Ability to show change/comparison
- Interaction potential
- Ethical clarity
- Risk of overclaiming

Do not choose the most sensational direction. Choose the one with the strongest evidence and clearest question.

# 13. Recommended research sequence

**Phase 1 — Frame**
Topic → scope → audience → research questions.

**Phase 2 — Evidence audit**
Collect external reports, peer-reviewed studies and published datasets.

**Phase 3 — Data preparation**
Inspect supplied CSV → document provenance → clean → derive variables → create data dictionary.

**Phase 4 — Primary data**
Run student survey → collect open-ended responses → interview 8–12 students.

**Phase 5 — Explore**
Descriptive statistics → cross-tabs → correlations → qualitative coding → compare sources.

**Phase 6 — Challenge your own assumptions**
Look specifically for evidence that contradicts the initial “AI is helpful vs harmful” framing.

**Phase 7 — Story testing**
Generate 4–6 narrative directions and evaluate them against evidence quality.

**Phase 8 — Final research conclusion**
Select one direction for Assignment 2 and document why.

Only after these phases should the interactive web page become the main design task.
